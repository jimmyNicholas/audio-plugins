# Research: what makes a drum groove feel good

Researched 2026-11-15 for the second Bloodline prototype. The user has sketched building blocks (effects, drums, bass, synth, patterns, racks), with patterns split into triggered (beat, fill, roll, one-drum part), pitched (bass line, riff, melody, arp, chords) and feel (swing, humanise, accent). A feel block changes a pattern the way an effect changes a sound.

The question: what makes a drum groove feel good, in the sense of feel rather than genre or "where the kick and snare go"? Which parts of feel (syncopation, accents, microtiming, swing, dynamics, ghost notes, density) matter, how do they interact, and which should a Bloodline user turn directly, which should be bred, and which should be lockable?

## How to read this

- **Method caveat:** full page reads were blocked in this session. `journals.plos.org`, `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` and `www.frontiersin.org` did not resolve, so per the session's network rules no further sites were tried. Every claim below comes from search-engine extracts of the cited pages (abstracts, publisher records, repository records and citing papers), not a full read of the paper. These are marked **[extract]**. Where the extract came from a later paper describing the cited one, rather than from the cited paper's own abstract, it is marked **[extract, second-hand]**. The researcher's arithmetic and design reasoning are marked **[inference]**. Anything recalled without a source found in this session is marked **[background, unverified]**. Claims read from this repository's own files are marked **[read]**.
- **Open access:** most of the papers below are open access (PLOS ONE, Frontiers, Scientific Reports, PMC). With those hosts allowed, a later pass could read the methods and numbers directly. The section "Sources not reached" lists what most needs checking.
- **Terms:**
  - **Groove** in this literature means "the sensation of wanting to move some part of your body in relation to some aspect of the music" (Madison 2006), usually with pleasure attached (Janata et al. 2012). It is measured by listener ratings, sometimes by movement, tapping, brain imaging or pupil size.
  - **Microtiming** is small, systematic deviation of onsets from the grid (a few to tens of milliseconds). **Swing** here means the long-short split of a pair of subdivisions. **Syncopation** is a note on a weak position that is not followed by one on the next stronger position.
  - **Swing percent** is the MPC convention used in Bloodline Breaks: where the second sixteenth (or eighth) lands inside its pair, so 50% is straight, 66.7% is a 2:1 triplet feel, 75% is 3:1. A ratio r:1 is r / (1 + r) as a percent **[inference]**.
- **Confidence:** High = several independent studies agree. Medium = one strong study, or studies that mostly agree. Low = one study, a disputed result, or theory without direct tests.
- **Scope:** feel, not sound. The [boom bap drum sound note](boom-bap-drum-sound.md) and [hi-hat synthesis note](hi-hat-synthesis.md) cover the voices. This note is not about the existing plugins, but section 10 relates the findings to them.

## TL;DR

| # | Finding | Confidence |
|---|---------|------------|
| 1 | **Groove is the pleasurable urge to move**, and listeners agree about it a lot, even across experience levels and with unfamiliar music. High-groove music is easier to tap to and draws more spontaneous movement. | High |
| 2 | **Syncopation follows an inverted U:** a medium amount gives the most wanting to move and pleasure; none is dull, too much is hard to follow. Shown with funk drum breaks, synthesised melodies, brain imaging and pupil size. But a 2024 study of 40 real pop drum patterns found **no effect of perceived complexity** on the urge to move, so the U is real for syncopation in controlled stimuli and doubtful as a general law for "complexity". | High (syncopation U) / Medium (generality) |
| 3 | **Microtiming does not reliably add groove.** Quantised versions are rated as groovy as, or groovier than, the original performances in most listening studies; exaggerated microtiming lowers groove. Only small or specific effects survive: expert listeners moving more to partly reduced deviations, and systematic jazz soloist downbeat delays of about 30 ms adding swing. Random jitter does not help. | High (no general benefit) / Medium (specific effects) |
| 4 | **Swing ratio is not a constant; it falls with tempo.** Jazz ride cymbal ratios ran from about 3.5:1 at slow tempos to near 1:1 at fast tempos, and 2:1 appeared only around one tempo. A replication with expert drummers agreed that swing timing does not scale proportionally with tempo. | High |
| 5 | **Timing and sound are not separable.** Drummers signal laid-back or pushed feel with loudness, duration and brightness as well as onset time, and the perceived "position" of a beat is a window (Danielsen's beat bin) whose width depends on the sound. A louder, longer, softer-attack hit can feel later without moving. | Medium |
| 6 | **Density helps, within limits.** Event density between beats and beat salience were the strongest audio correlates of groove across genres; in real drum patterns syncopation and density each explained only about 1% of groove variance, while liking the style explained about 12%. More instruments and staggered entrances also raised groove. | Medium |
| 7 | **Human timing fluctuation has structure.** Real drummers' timing and loudness show long-range correlations (fractal, 1/f-like drift), not white noise, and listeners preferred long-range correlated humanising over the uncorrelated randomness DAW "humanise" functions use. | Medium |
| 8 | **No controlled study isolating ghost notes was found.** Evidence on velocity comes from performance analysis (accent patterns per phrase, laid-back strokes played louder) and from studies where loudness is part of feel. Ghost notes are best treated as a density and accent device, with their effect on groove untested. | Low |

## 1. What groove is, and why the definition matters

- **Definition and agreement.** Madison (2006) rated 64 commercial music examples and found considerable agreement between listeners on how much each made them want to move **[extract]**. Later work cites it for listeners agreeing "even when they have widely varying musical experience and are presented with unfamiliar music" **[extract, second-hand]**. Madison, G. (2006). Experiencing groove induced by music: consistency and phenomenology. *Music Perception* 24(2), 201-208. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2006.24.2.201 . **Confidence:** High.
- **Pleasure plus movement.** Across several samples, people's own definitions of groove converged on "an aspect of music that evokes a pleasurable urge to move". High-groove music drew more spontaneous head and foot movement and tighter tapping synchronisation than low-groove music, and was felt as easier to tap to **[extract, second-hand]**. Janata, P., Tomic, S. T. & Haberman, J. M. (2012). Sensorimotor coupling in music and the psychology of the groove. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 141(1), 54-75. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024208 ; preprint copy https://zenodo.org/record/997607 . **Confidence:** High.
- **Two correlated parts.** The Experience of Groove Questionnaire separates **urge to move** and **pleasure** as two correlated factors (three items each, reliabilities .92 and .97) **[extract]**. Senn, O., Bechtold, T., Rose, D., Câmara, G. S. et al. (2020). Experience of Groove Questionnaire: instrument development and initial validation. *Music Perception* 38(1), 46-65. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2020.38.1.46 . **Confidence:** High. **Takeaway [inference]:** a pattern can make you move without much pleasure, or please without much movement. Harmony moves pleasure more than movement (section 7).

## 2. Syncopation

### 2.1 The inverted U

- **Funk drum breaks.** Listeners rated 50 drum breaks (36 taken from real tracks) for wanting to move and pleasure. Medium syncopation gave the most of both, especially for people who enjoy dancing; audio entropy was a poor predictor. Syncopation was measured with a variant of the Longuet-Higgins and Lee (1984) measure. A correction was published in 2015 (doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0139409), not checked here **[extract]**. Witek, M. A. G., Clarke, E. F., Wallentin, M., Kringelbach, M. L. & Vuust, P. (2014). Syncopation, body-movement and pleasure in groove music. *PLOS ONE* 9(4), e94446. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446 . **Confidence:** High (the U) / Low (sample size, reported only by press as "over 60").
- **Rhythm and harmony together.** About 200 online participants rated stimuli crossing rhythmic and harmonic complexity. Groove followed an inverted U for rhythm. Pleasure mediated the effect of harmony on wanting to move, and very complex chords flattened the effect of rhythm on pleasure **[extract]**. Matthews, T. E., Witek, M. A. G., Heggli, O. A., Penhune, V. B. & Vuust, P. (2019). The sensation of groove is affected by the interaction of rhythmic and harmonic complexity. *PLOS ONE* 14(1), e0204539. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0204539 . **Confidence:** High.
- **In the brain.** In fMRI, medium-complexity rhythms drew higher pleasure and wanting-to-move ratings and activity in beat and reward regions; basal ganglia activity (nucleus accumbens, caudate, putamen) tracked the ratings **[extract]**. Matthews, T. E., Witek, M. A. G., Lund, T., Vuust, P. & Penhune, V. B. (2020). The sensation of groove engages motor and reward networks. *NeuroImage* 214, 116768. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.116768 . **Confidence:** Medium (one imaging study).
- **In attention.** Moderately complex drum rhythms held attention longer (slower pupil drift) than simpler or more complex ones. Syncopations and pickups had complementary effects: syncopation on groove ratings, pickups on pupil size **[extract]**. Spiech, C., Sioros, G., Endestad, T., Danielsen, A. & Laeng, B. (2022). Pupil drift rate indexes groove ratings. *Scientific Reports* 12. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-15763-w . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **In synthesised melodies.** With rule-based shifting of notes to create syncopation, and density varied as a control, syncopation correlated with groove **[extract]**. Sioros, G., Miron, M., Davies, M., Gouyon, F. & Madison, G. (2014). Syncopation creates the sensation of groove in synthesized music examples. *Frontiers in Psychology* 5, 1036. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01036 . **Confidence:** Medium. Note this showed a rising relationship in its range, not a full U.
- **What musicians do.** Asked to maximise groove in simple melodies, musicians moved note onsets and offsets to faster metrical levels (that is, syncopated them), which raised groove to the level of the complex melodies **[extract, second-hand]**. Madison, G. & Sioros, G. (2014). What musicians do to induce the sensation of groove in simple and complex melodies, and how listeners perceive it. *Frontiers in Psychology* 5, 894. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00894 . **Confidence:** Medium.

### 2.2 Where the U weakens

- **Movement is not ratings.** With motion capture of free movement to drum breaks, medium syncopation gave about the same acceleration and synchronisation as low syncopation; people who enjoy dancing made more complex movements, and the torso synchronised more closely to the beat than the hands **[extract]**. Witek, M. A. G. et al. (2017). Syncopation affects free body-movement in musical groove. *Experimental Brain Research* 235(4), 995-1005. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-016-4855-6 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Complexity is not syncopation.** On 40 idiomatic pop drum patterns (kick, snare and cymbals only) with complexity measured in a separate pairwise experiment (220 listeners, 4,400 trials), 179 participants showed **no significant effect of complexity on the urge to move**; the inverted U was not confirmed. The authors suggest simple patterns invite dancing by metric clarity and complex ones by interest **[extract]**. Senn, O., Hoesl, F., Bechtold, T. A., Kilchenmann, L., Jerjen, R. & Witek, M. A. G. (2024). Null effect of perceived drum pattern complexity on the experience of groove. *PLOS ONE* 19(11), e0311877. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0311877 ; stimulus set: Senn, O. et al. (2023). A stimulus set of 40 popular music drum patterns with perceived complexity measures. *Music & Science* 6. https://doi.org/10.1177/20592043231202576 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Small effect in real patterns.** Across 248 reconstructed drum patterns (665 participants, 8,329 ratings), syncopation was positively associated with groove but explained only about 1% of variance (R² = 0.010) **[extract]**. Senn, O., Kilchenmann, L., Bechtold, T. & Hoesl, F. (2018). Groove in drum patterns as a function of both rhythmic properties and listeners' attitudes. *PLOS ONE* 13(6), e0199604. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199604 . **Confidence:** High.
- **Culture.** A study of Ghanaian and American university students and staff argues that exposure shapes synchronisation and that how syncopation is defined depends on cultural and musical context; effect sizes were not reached **[extract]**. Witek, M. A. G., Liu, J., Kuubertzie, J., Yankyera, A. P., Adzei, S. & Vuust, P. (2020). A critical cross-cultural study of sensorimotor and groove responses to syncopation among Ghanaian and American university students and staff. *Music Perception* 37(4), 278-297. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2020.37.4.278 . **Confidence:** Low (findings not read).

**Takeaway [inference]:** syncopation is the best-supported single lever, but real, idiomatic patterns mostly already sit in a reasonable range. Pushing a pattern from very square to moderately syncopated helps; fine differences between already good patterns matter far less than whether the listener likes the style.

## 3. Accent and metrical weight

- **The Longuet-Higgins and Lee measure.** Each position gets a metrical weight by the highest level of the metre that starts there. A syncopation is a rest or tied note on a stronger position than the sounded note before it, and its size is the weight difference. Listeners assume a passage is unsyncopated if they can read it that way **[extract]**. Longuet-Higgins, H. C. & Lee, C. S. (1984). The rhythmic interpretation of monophonic music. *Music Perception* 1(4), 424-441. https://doi.org/10.2307/40285271 . Smith and Honing (2006) note the model had never been empirically validated at the time **[extract]** (https://www.mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/papers/smith-honing-2006.pdf). The usual weights are 0 for the bar, -1 half bar, -2 beat, -3 eighth, -4 sixteenth **[background, unverified]**; a Python implementation is in the syncopation dataset code at https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/hg/syncopation-dataset/annotate/76ce27beba95/Syncopation%20models/LHL.py . **Confidence:** High (definition) / Medium (validity).
- **Too much makes the beat move.** In tapping and reproduction tasks, listeners tended to reset the phase of their internal pulse with highly syncopated rhythms, hearing them as less syncopated, and simpler rhythms were remembered better **[extract]**. Fitch, W. T. & Rosenfeld, A. J. (2007). Perception and production of syncopated rhythms. *Music Perception* 25(1), 43-58. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2007.25.1.43 . **Confidence:** High. **Takeaway [inference]:** this is the mechanism for the right-hand side of the U: past some point the listener re-hears the downbeat, and the syncopation stops being felt as syncopation.
- **Which drum carries it matters.** In a drum kit, how destabilising a syncopation is depends on how many streams there are, which instrument plays it and where in the bar it falls, interacting with training **[extract]**. Witek, M. A. G., Clarke, E. F., Kringelbach, M. L. & Vuust, P. (2014). Effects of polyphonic context, instrumentation, and metrical location on syncopation in music. *Music Perception* 32(2), 201-217. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2014.32.2.201 . The per-instrument results were not reached. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **A drum-kit syncopation index.** Hoesl and Senn's revised index of syncopation, derived from Witek et al.'s measure, correlated with perceived complexity at r = .701 and fitted complexity data with R² = .560 in later work **[extract, second-hand]**; see Senn et al. 2023 above, and Senn et al. (2023), A predictive coding approach to modelling the perceived complexity of popular music drum patterns, *Heliyon* (record https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsdoj,edsdoj.b7d8b052f0b14e8cbdbbab3ec4405f9d ). The weights were not reached. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Bass carries time.** Timing deviations were better encoded for the lower of two simultaneous tones (larger mismatch negativity), and tapping followed the lower stream more; modelling put the cause in the cochlea **[extract]**. Hove, M. J., Marie, C., Bruce, I. C. & Trainor, L. J. (2014). Superior time perception for lower musical pitch explains why bass-ranged instruments lay down musical rhythms. *PNAS* 111(28), 10383-10388. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1402039111 . **Confidence:** High. **Takeaway [inference]:** a kick (or bass) off the grid is felt more than a hat off the grid, so kick syncopation should weigh more in any measure, and kick timing is the least safe thing to randomise.
- **Pressing's framing.** Pressing describes groove as a "kinetic framework for reliable prediction of events and time patterns", with Black Atlantic music favouring equal pulses and metronomic timing without rubato, so that syncopation and offbeat phrasing work against a steady frame **[extract, second-hand]**. Pressing, J. (2002). Black Atlantic rhythm: its computational and transcultural foundations. *Music Perception* 19(3), 285-310. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2002.19.3.285 . **Confidence:** Medium (theory).

## 4. Microtiming

This is where the research most clearly disagrees with musicians' lore.

### 4.1 The theory: participatory discrepancies

- **Keil.** Music's power comes from small discrepancies between players, "processual" (timing) and "textural" (sound), that invite participation **[extract]**. Keil, C. (1987). Participatory discrepancies and the power of music. *Cultural Anthropology* 2(3), 275-283. https://doi.org/10.1525/can.1987.2.3.02a00010 ; full text copy https://www.negrophonic.com/pdfs/Keil_Participatory%20Discrepancies_CA_1987.pdf . Reprinted in Keil and Feld, *Music Grooves* (1994). **Confidence:** High (what Keil claims) / not an empirical result.
- **Butterfield's test.** Most listeners could not consistently identify discrepancies of 30 ms or less, about the size of those in real performance, which gives little support to the theory **[extract, second-hand]**. Butterfield, M. W. (2010). Participatory discrepancies and the perception of beats in jazz. *Music Perception* 27(3), 157-176. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2010.27.3.157 . **Confidence:** Medium.

### 4.2 Studies that found no benefit, or harm

- **Across genres.** Event density between beats and beat salience correlated strongly with groove across genres; microtiming "did not play any significant role" **[extract]**. Madison, G., Gouyon, F., Ullén, F. & Hörnström, K. (2011). Modeling the tendency for music to induce movement in humans: first correlations with low-level audio descriptors across music genres. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 37(5), 1578-1594. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024323 ; preprint https://repositorio.inesctec.pt/handle/123456789/1887 . **Confidence:** High.
- **Short rhythms.** Jazz, funk and samba patterns with idiomatic microtiming scaled from none to about double: systematic microtiming lowered groove, liking and naturalness, except for the simple short-long jazz shuffle; experts were more affected **[extract, second-hand]**. Davies, M., Madison, G., Silva, P. & Gouyon, F. (2013). The effect of microtiming deviations on the perception of groove in short rhythms. *Music Perception* 30(5), 497-510. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2013.30.5.497 ; repository https://repositorio.inesctec.pt/handle/123456789/3901 . **Confidence:** High.
- **A rock pattern.** Displacing hits of a rock drum pattern by 15 or 25 ms either way: the quantised version was not beaten, and larger displacement lowered groove **[extract, second-hand]**. Frühauf, J., Kopiez, R. & Platz, F. (2013). Music on the timing grid: the influence of microtiming on the perceived groove quality of a simple drum pattern performance. *Musicae Scientiae* 17(2), 246-260. https://doi.org/10.1177/1029864913486793 . **Confidence:** Medium (own abstract not reached).
- **Real performances, scaled.** Professional swing and funk bass and drum recordings, with the played deviations scaled: groove was high for deviations equal to or smaller than those played and fell for exaggerated ones; fully quantised and original were rated equally high; experts were more sensitive. Neither the participatory discrepancy claim nor the "exactitude" claim (that any deviation hurts) was supported **[extract]**. Senn, O., Kilchenmann, L., von Georgi, R. & Bullerjahn, C. (2016). The effect of expert performance microtiming on listeners' experience of groove in swing or funk music. *Frontiers in Psychology* 7, 1487. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487 . **Confidence:** High.
- **Real drum patterns.** In the 248-pattern study, residual microtiming, beat salience and rhythmic variability had no effect on groove **[extract]** (Senn et al. 2018, above). **Confidence:** High.
- **Jazz piano swing.** Quantising, doubling or inverting a pianist's microtiming: quantised versions were rated slightly more swinging than originals on average, doubled deviations least swinging, inverting hurt only two pieces (160 musicians) **[extract]**. Datseris, G., Ziereis, A., Albrecht, T., Hagmayer, Y., Priesemann, V. & Geisel, T. (2019). Microtiming deviations and swing feel in jazz. *Scientific Reports* 9, 19824. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-55981-3 ; preprint https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.03442 . **Confidence:** High.

### 4.3 Studies that found a benefit

- **Movement in experts.** In the same swing and funk material as Senn et al. 2016 (160 participants, motion capture), expert listeners moved more to clips with partly reduced deviations than to fully quantised ones: a small to moderate effect, experts only, read as partial support for participatory discrepancies **[extract]**. Kilchenmann, L. & Senn, O. (2015). Microtiming in swing and funk affects the body movement behavior of music expert listeners. *Frontiers in Psychology* 6, 1232. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01232 . A follow-up reports that the loosest timing triggered strong entrainment in experts **[extract, second-hand]**. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Downbeat delays in jazz.** Across 456 jazz solos, almost all soloists delayed downbeats relative to the rhythm section, by about 30 ms on average and less at faster tempos, while keeping offbeats in sync. Musicians rated versions with delayed downbeats and synchronised offbeats as most swinging; random timing deviations did not help and could hurt **[extract]**. Nelias, C., Sturm, E. M., Albrecht, T., Hagmayer, Y. & Geisel, T. (2022). Downbeat delays are a key component of swing in jazz. *Communications Physics* 5, 237. https://doi.org/10.1038/s42005-022-00995-z . **Confidence:** Medium (one group; and it is between instruments, not within a drum part).

### 4.4 Structured, not random

- **Humanise should be correlated.** Human rhythmic performance (including drummers) shows long-range correlated fluctuations. DAW humanise functions are "essentially random number generators producing only simple uncorrelated fluctuations". 39 listeners preferred long-range correlated humanising over white noise **[extract]**. Hennig, H., Fleischmann, R., Fredebohm, A., Hagmayer, Y., Nagler, J., Witt, A., Theis, F. J. & Geisel, T. (2011). The nature and perception of fluctuations in human musical rhythms. *PLOS ONE* 6(10), e26457. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026457 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **A real drummer.** Jeff Porcaro's one-handed sixteenth hi-hat on "I Keep Forgettin'" shows long-range correlations in both inter-hit intervals and hit loudness, short-range anticorrelation from one hit to the next (attributed to motor delays), a pulse that drifts with the song's sections, and a distinct loudness pattern for each two-bar phrase **[extract]**. Räsänen, E., Pulkkinen, O., Virtanen, T., Zollner, M. & Hennig, H. (2015). Fluctuations of hi-hat timing and dynamics in a virtuoso drum track of a popular music recording. *PLOS ONE* 10(6), e0127902. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0127902 . **Confidence:** Medium (one drummer, one track).

**Takeaway [inference]:** across studies, deviations at or below performed size are neutral, larger ones hurt, random ones do not help, and the few positive effects are systematic (a consistent late downbeat, a consistent relationship between parts). "Feel" offsets should be consistent per part and per position, not per hit noise.

## 5. Swing

- **Ratio falls with tempo.** Ride cymbal swing ratios on three well-known recordings and a play-along record ran from about 3.5:1 at slow tempos to about 1:1 at fast tempos; 2:1 appeared only at one tempo. At slow tempos soloists played downbeats after the cymbal but met it on offbeats, so soloists swung less than the drummer **[extract]**. Friberg, A. & Sundström, A. (2002). Swing ratios and ensemble timing in jazz performance: evidence for a common rhythmic pattern. *Music Perception* 19(3), 333-349. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2002.19.3.333 ; summary page https://www.speech.kth.se/music/performance/Texts/ensemble_swing.htm . A roughly constant short-note duration across medium to fast tempos is often attributed to this paper **[background, unverified]**. **Confidence:** High.
- **Replicated with expert drummers.** Expert jazz drummers keep tight control of timing, and the swing ratio shifts systematically with tempo rather than scaling proportionally **[extract]**. Honing, H. & de Haas, W. B. (2008). Swing once more: relating timing and tempo in expert jazz drumming. *Music Perception* 25(5), 471-476. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2008.25.5.471 ; author copy https://mcg.uva.nl/mcg-2023/papers/honing-haas-2008.pdf . **Confidence:** High.
- **Wide range in practice.** Benadon's beat-upbeat ratio (BUR) measurements of jazz eighths range from 1.0 through 1.5 and 2.0 to 3.0, and swing is treated as expressive microrhythm rather than one value **[extract]**. Benadon, F. (2006). Slicing the beat: jazz eighth-notes as expressive microrhythm. *Ethnomusicology* 50(1), 73-98. https://doi.org/10.2307/20174424 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Why drummers swing.** Butterfield argues the drummers' more uneven, triplet-like eighths help listeners perceive the quarter-note beat, while straighter soloist eighths keep forward momentum **[extract]**. Butterfield, M. W. (2011). Why do jazz musicians swing their eighth notes? *Music Theory Spectrum* 33(1), 3-26. https://doi.org/10.1525/mts.2011.33.1.3 . **Confidence:** Low (argument, not experiment).
- **Tempo itself.** Drum breaks at six tempos from 60 to 200 BPM had an optimal tempo for groove at about 100 to 120 BPM **[extract]**. Etani, T., Marui, A., Kawase, S. & Keller, P. E. (2018). Optimal tempo for groove: its relation to directions of body movement and Japanese nori. *Frontiers in Psychology* 9, 462. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00462 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **In MPC terms [inference]:** 3.5:1 is about 78%, 3:1 is 75%, 2:1 is 66.7%, 1.5:1 is 60%. The jazz data are swung eighths on a ride; hip hop and house swing sixteenths, and no study found here measured how sixteenth swing should vary with tempo. The direction (less swing as the subdivision gets faster in absolute time) is the transferable part, not the numbers.

## 6. Dynamics, accents and ghost notes

- **Feel is also sound.** 22 professional drummers played a simple backbeat and a complex variation, laid back, on the beat and pushed. They used duration, intensity and brightness as well as timing to convey the feels; in the related thesis, longer snare duration and higher snare and hi-hat intensity marked laid-back and pushed strokes **[extract]**. Câmara, G. S., Nymoen, K., Lartillot, O. & Danielsen, A. (2020). Timing is everything... or is it? Effects of instructed timing style, reference and pattern on drum kit sound in groove-based performance. *Music Perception* 38(1), 1-26. https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2020.38.1.1 ; repository copy https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/79965 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Laid back is louder.** Ten drummers playing a rock backbeat at 64, 96 and 148 BPM: most played laid-back snare strokes louder than on-the-beat strokes, and faster tempos raised level and lowered brightness (spectral centroid) **[extract]**. Danielsen, A., Waadeland, C. H., Sundt, H. G. & Witek, M. A. G. (2015). Effects of instructed timing and tempo on snare drum sound in drum kit performance. *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 138(4), 2301-2316. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4930950 ; accepted manuscript https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/55787 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **The beat is a window.** Danielsen models each beat as a "beat bin" with extent in time rather than a point; its width depends on the sounds (transient sharpness, bass) and the listener's tolerance, and close onsets merge into one wide beat **[extract, second-hand]**. Danielsen, A. (ed.) (2010). *Musical Rhythm in the Age of Digital Reproduction*. Ashgate (review https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/dancecult/dancecult-366.htm ); RITMO TIME project https://www.uio.no/ritmo/english/projects/time/ . Her earlier book on James Brown and Parliament treats the funk groove as a structural unit heard as a whole **[extract, second-hand]**: Danielsen, A. (2006). *Presence and Pleasure: The Funk Grooves of James Brown and Parliament*. Wesleyan University Press. **Confidence:** Medium (theory, well grounded in analysis; perceptual tests ongoing).
- **Loudness has structure too.** In the Porcaro hi-hat, hit amplitudes were long-range correlated and each two-bar phrase had its own accent pattern **[extract]** (Räsänen et al. 2015, above). **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Ghost notes.** No listening experiment isolating ghost notes (quiet off-beat strokes, typically on snare) and groove was found in this search. **Confidence:** Low, absence of evidence. **Takeaway [inference]:** a ghost note adds an event between beats (density, section 7) without adding a strong accent, so it raises density while keeping the backbeat salient. That combination matches the two strongest correlates in Madison et al. 2011, which is a plausible reason they work, but untested.

## 7. Density, instrumentation and the listener

- **Density and beat salience.** Event density between beats and beat salience were the two descriptors most strongly correlated with groove across genres **[extract]** (Madison et al. 2011, above). **Confidence:** High.
- **Small in real drum patterns.** Density explained about 1% of groove variance (R² = 0.011) in 248 real patterns, like syncopation **[extract]** (Senn et al. 2018). **Confidence:** High.
- **More parts, staggered entrances.** Music with more instrument parts got higher groove ratings; staggered entrances raised both sensorimotor coupling and groove; training did not change this **[extract]**. Hurley, B. K., Martens, P. A. & Janata, P. (2014). Spontaneous sensorimotor coupling with multipart music. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 40(4), 1679-1696. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037154 ; author copy https://bkhurley.github.io/assets/HurleyMartensJanata_2014_JEPHPP.pdf . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **The listener matters most.** In real drum patterns, the strongest single effect on groove was style bias: high ratings when listeners thought the pattern belonged to a style they liked (R² = 0.123, about ten times syncopation or density) **[extract]** (Senn et al. 2018). Liking to dance strengthened the syncopation effect (Witek et al. 2014) and wanting-to-move ratings (Matthews et al. 2019). **Confidence:** High.
- **Harmony shifts pleasure.** Harmony acts mainly on the pleasure side of groove (Matthews et al. 2019, above). **Confidence:** Medium.

## 8. Why: predictive coding

- **The account.** Vuust and Witek propose that the brain predicts rhythm against a metric model; syncopation produces prediction errors against that model, and groove and its pleasure come from a balance between the model holding and being challenged **[extract]**. Vuust, P. & Witek, M. A. G. (2014). Rhythmic complexity and predictive coding: a novel approach to modeling rhythm and meter perception in music. *Frontiers in Psychology* 5, 1111. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01111 . **Confidence:** Medium (theory, consistent with the data).
- **The sweet spot.** Groove depends on "a fine-tuned interplay between predictability arising from repetitive rhythmic patterns, and surprise arising from rhythmic deviations"; moderately syncopated rhythms produce the most strongly weighted prediction errors, since low complexity is boring and high complexity cannot be modelled **[extract]**. Stupacher, J., Matthews, T. E., Pando-Naude, V., Foster Vander Elst, O. & Vuust, P. (2022). The sweet spot between predictability and surprise: musical groove in brain, body, and social interactions. *Frontiers in Psychology* 13, 906190. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.906190 . **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Fit with the other findings [inference]:** the account explains why syncopation helps (surprise against a stable metre), why too much hurts (the metre resets, Fitch and Rosenfeld 2007), why beat salience and bass matter (they hold the model up), why repetition matters (it builds the prediction), and why random microtiming does not help (noise is not informative surprise). It does not explain the null result for complexity in real pop patterns, unless those patterns all sit near the top of the curve already.

## 9. Where the research disagrees

| Question | One side | Other side | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does microtiming add groove? | Keil 1987 (theory); Kilchenmann and Senn 2015 (experts move more to partly reduced deviations than quantised); Nelias et al. 2022 (systematic downbeat delays add swing) | Madison et al. 2011; Davies et al. 2013; Frühauf et al. 2013; Butterfield 2010; Senn et al. 2016 and 2018; Datseris et al. 2019 (quantised as good or better; exaggerated worse) | No general benefit. Systematic, small, between-part offsets may help, mainly for experts. Random jitter does not. |
| Is there an inverted U? | Witek et al. 2014; Matthews et al. 2019, 2020; Spiech et al. 2022; Stupacher et al. 2022 | Senn et al. 2024 (no effect of perceived complexity in real pop patterns); Witek et al. 2017 (free movement did not peak at medium); Sioros et al. 2014 (rising, not a U, in its range) | Strong for syncopation in controlled stimuli and ratings; weak for "complexity" in general and for real movement. |
| Does syncopation matter much? | Inverted-U studies | Senn et al. 2018: R² about 0.01 in real patterns; style bias about 0.12 | Large across the full range from square to chaotic, small among idiomatic patterns. |
| Is swing a fixed ratio? | MPC-style percentages treat it as one number | Friberg and Sundström 2002; Honing and de Haas 2008; Benadon 2006 | It varies with tempo and between players and parts. |
| Is timing just timing? | Most microtiming studies vary onset only | Câmara et al. 2020; Danielsen et al. 2015; Danielsen 2010 | Perceived timing depends on loudness, duration and brightness; onset-only studies may miss how feel is made. |

## 10. Relating it to Bloodline's building blocks

Everything in this section is **suggestion [inference]**, built on the findings above, not a finding.

### 10.1 What the existing prototypes do (for reference)

- Bloodline Breaks has a **swing dial** (50% to 75%, MPC style) that pulls each bar's swing towards it, **styles** that pull where hits land, how hard, how late and whether hats roll, and **Pull** (loose to tight) for how hard both pull. The inspector edits steps as hit, ghost or rest. [plugins/bloodline-breaks/README.md](../../plugins/bloodline-breaks/README.md) **[read]**
- Bloodline Breaks 2 adds free placement off the grid, velocity drawn as height, and Swing, Pull and Spread dials that steer the next breed. [plugins/bloodline-breaks-2/README.md](../../plugins/bloodline-breaks-2/README.md) **[read]**

### 10.2 Dials, breeding, locks

| Aspect | Suggested role | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Swing amount** | **Dial** on the feel block, with a tempo-aware mode | Listeners and players treat it as one obvious quantity; it changes with tempo in real playing (Friberg and Sundström 2002; Honing and de Haas 2008), so a user wants to set it for the track, not have it drift each round. |
| **Tempo** | Outside breeding, a host or rack setting | It sets the swing ratio and has its own optimum (Etani et al. 2018); breeding it would confound everything else. |
| **Syncopation** | **Bred**, with a **target band** the user can move | The strongest pattern-level lever (Witek et al. 2014), but small among already good patterns (Senn et al. 2018). Breeding finds where in the band; the band stops breeding drifting to square or chaotic. |
| **Density** | **Bred**, with a lockable range | Correlates with groove (Madison et al. 2011) but trades off with beat salience; a ceiling keeps the beat clear. |
| **Which positions are accented (accent pattern)** | **Bred** as part of the pattern, **lockable** per lane | Accents define metrical weight and carry the backbeat; losing the backbeat to breeding destroys beat salience. Lock the "anchor" hits (for example kick on 1, snare on 2 and 4) when wanted. |
| **Accent depth (how loud accents are against the rest)** | **Dial** on the feel block | A global contrast, like a compressor's ratio; real drummers vary it with tempo and feel (Danielsen et al. 2015). |
| **Ghost notes (count, placement)** | **Bred** as part of density | Treated as quiet density events (section 6); untested, so let taste decide. |
| **Ghost level** | **Dial** | One clear number a user wants to set by ear. |
| **Per-part offset (laid back or pushed snare, kick on top)** | **Dial per lane** on the feel block, small range | The positive microtiming results are systematic, per part (Nelias et al. 2022; Câmara et al. 2020). Keep the range modest (tens of milliseconds); exaggerated deviation hurts (Senn et al. 2016; Davies et al. 2013). |
| **Humanise (fluctuation)** | **Dial** for amount, with structured (1/f-like) noise, **locked out of breeding** | Random jitter does not help (Nelias et al. 2022) and correlated drift is preferred (Hennig et al. 2011). It is a texture, not a pattern trait; breeding it would make candidates differ for reasons the user cannot hear in one bar. |
| **Kick timing** | Lock tightest by default | Low sounds carry the timing (Hove et al. 2014); kick deviations are heard most. |
| **Style** | Soft pull, as now | Style liking is the largest single effect on groove (Senn et al. 2018); the user's taste is the target, which breeding already learns. |

### 10.3 Design ideas

1. **A syncopation meter and band.** Compute a Longuet-Higgins and Lee style syncopation score per bar (weights by metrical level, summed over syncopated positions), weighted by drum: kick more than snare more than hats, after Hove et al. 2014 and Witek et al. 2014 (Music Perception). Show it as a small meter on each candidate. Let the breeder reject or down-weight offspring outside a user-set band (default: medium). The inverted U says the middle is safest; the 2024 null result says do not force a single peak, so make it a band, not a target value.
2. **Keep the downbeat findable.** Since the metre resets under heavy syncopation (Fitch and Rosenfeld 2007), require at least one strong anchor per bar (a kick or accent on beat 1, or the backbeat) unless the user unlocks it. This keeps beat salience, which Madison et al. 2011 found matters more than microtiming.
3. **Tempo-dependent swing.** Let the swing dial set swing at a reference tempo (say 90 BPM) and scale it down as tempo rises, following the direction of Friberg and Sundström 2002 and Honing and de Haas 2008. One option: keep the short note's absolute length roughly constant over a tempo range, clamped between 50% and the dial's value. The exact curve for sixteenth swing is not in the literature found, so tune by ear and offer a "fixed" switch for MPC-style programming.
4. **Feel offsets as consistent per-lane shifts, not per-hit noise.** A "laid back snare" is one number for the lane (maybe 0 to 30 ms late), applied the same way each bar, optionally with matching loudness and length changes (Câmara et al. 2020; Danielsen et al. 2015): laid back means a bit later, louder and longer together. That makes feel a sound-and-time macro, which suits Bloodline's effect-like feel block.
5. **Structured humanise.** Generate timing and velocity drift with pink (1/f) noise or a random walk with a pull back to centre, plus a small negative correlation from one hit to the next, after Hennig et al. 2011 and Räsänen et al. 2015. Keep it small by default and off for the kick. Never breed it.
6. **Accent pattern per phrase.** Räsänen et al. 2015 found a distinct loudness shape per two-bar phrase. A feel block could carry a two-bar accent contour that repeats, separate from the per-hit pattern, so loops do not feel like one bar copied.
7. **Style beats complexity.** Because liking the style is the biggest effect (Senn et al. 2018), the learned taste in Bloodline Breaks is the right main steer. The measures above are guard rails, not goals.
8. **Experts hear microtiming; most people do not.** Senn et al. 2016 and Kilchenmann and Senn 2015 found experts more sensitive. Fine offset dials can live behind an inspector or a "more" view, so they do not crowd the main screen.
